Abstract 

We find that explicit reasoning does not necessarily translate into better multimodal emotion recognition (MER) accuracy, even though it makes predictions more interpretable. Specifically, for reasoning-based MLLMs, fast thinking by triggering direct answers often outperforms slow thinking after deliberative reasoning. Our empirical analyses show that fast thinking improves recall with broader and more confident predictions, whereas slow thinking favors precision through conservative filtering of incorrect categories. Building on these insights, we propose MER-R1 ,a reinforcement learning framework that turns slow-fast complementarity into explicit optimization. Dual-objective disentanglement separates recall and precision into two optimization signals, allowing them to be jointly optimized rather than traded off against each other. Slow-fast confidence calibration further aligns the final slow-thinking answer with fast-thinking intuition, strengthening correct emotions while suppressing incorrect ones. In this way, MER-R1 unifies the recall-oriented intuition of fast thinking with the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking. We further provide theoretical justification for this synergy, showing that it mitigates variance-induced interference during optimization. Extensive experiments on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion show that MER-R1 achieves SoTA performance and makes reasoning genuinely benefit emotion recognition. 

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [ 1– 3] are transforming affective computing [ 4 –6 ], shifting multimodal emotion recognition [ 7– 10 ] from fixed-label prediction to 

Open-Vocabulary Multimodal Emotion Recognition (OV-MER) [ 11 , 12 ], where models recognize diverse and open-ended human emotions. To improve trustworthiness [ 13 ], recent RLVR-based approaches [ 14 – 16 ] further equip emotion MLLMs with explicit reasoning chains that ground emotion predictions in visual, acoustic, and textual cues. Despite this promise, we find that such reasoning does not necessarily lead to stronger emotion recognition. Specifically, we consider two thinking modes for an R1-like emotion reasoning model [ 3,15 ]: (1) Slow thinking produces an answer after standard reasoning process, and (2) Fast thinking 

elicits an answer directly without reasoning. Despite well-formed reasoning traces (Figure 1 (a)), slow thinking consistently underperforms fast thinking on current OV-MER benchmarks such as MER-UniBench [11, 12] (Figure 1 (b)). This reveals a thinking paradox in MER: 

Reasoning improves interpretability, yet fails to improve recognition accuracy itself.

To understand this paradox, we analyze fast and slow thinking in Section 3 from prediction and confi-dence perspectives. At the prediction level, fast thinking exhibits stronger System-1 intuition [ 17 ], producing broader emotion coverage and higher recall, whereas slow thinking makes more concen-trated predictions and favors precision. At the confidence level, fast thinking is more confident on correct emotions, while slow thinking more conservatively suppresses incorrect categories but may also lower confidence in correct ones. These findings suggest two slow-fast synergy desiderata for effective MER reasoning: preserving the recall-oriented coverage and correct-category confidence of fast thinking, while retaining the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking. To realize these desiderata, we propose MER-R1 , a reinforcement learning (RL) framework with two components. First, dual-objective disentanglement separates recall and precision into two optimization targets and preserves them in both reward and advantage spaces. Unlike prior MER reasoning methods that directly optimize F1 reward [ 15 ], our design prevents recall and precision interference during advantage normalization. Our theoretical analysis further shows that standard 

F1 optimization can be biased toward the objective with larger group-wise variation, whereas the dual-objective advantage establishes a balanced coupling to both recall and precision signals. Second, 

slow-fast confidence calibration transfers fast thinking’s confidence behavior to the final slow-thinking answer while preserving slow thinking’s selectivity. Specifically, we compare category-level confidence between the two thinking modes and calibrate correct and incorrect categories in opposite directions: correct categories are encouraged to retain or exceed fast-thinking confidence, whereas incorrect categories are encouraged to remain suppressed. This enables MER-R1 to strengthen correct emotions without absorbing the noisy over-coverage of fast thinking. 

Our contributions are three-fold: (1) Findings: We reveal a thinking paradox in MER: for reasoning-based MLLMs, fast thinking can outperform slow thinking. We diagnose this paradox from prediction and confidence perspectives and derive two desiderata for slow-fast synergy. (2) Methodology: We propose MER-R1 , an RL framework with dual-objective disentanglement and slow-fast confidence calibration, enabling joint recall–precision optimization and confidence calibration over correct and incorrect categories. (3) Performance: MER-R1 achieves state-of-the-art results on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion, and validates that satisfying the two slow-fast synergy desiderata makes reasoning genuinely beneficial for emotion recognition rather than merely improving interpretability. 

2 Related work 

MLLM reasoning. Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [ 1, 3 , 18 –20 ] have acquired reasoning ability through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) [ 14 , 17 , 21 ], allow-2ing them to explicitly reason over visual, acoustic, and textual signals during the reasoning process. Building on this capability, recent work [ 22 – 25 ] seeks to tackle increasingly complex multimodal reasoning tasks. However, recent studies suggest that slow thinking is not always better than fast thinking. VideoAuto-R1 [ 26 ] observes this phenomenon on video understanding tasks and addresses it with an answer-think-answer paradigm that rewards both the early and final answers while assigning a larger weight to the latter. However, this design remains at the output level and does not model why fast and slow thinking differ, let alone how to realize synergy between them. In contrast, our work studies their underlying complementarity and turns it into an explicit synergy framework for multimodal reasoning, thereby making slow thinking genuinely more effective. 

Multimodal emotion understanding. Multimodal emotion understanding has recently shifted from closed-set classification to more open and reasoning-oriented settings [ 11 , 13 , 27 , 28 ]. Open-Vocabulary Multimodal Emotion Recognition (OV-MER) [ 12 ] requires models to predict free-form emotion words beyond a fixed label space, while MME-Emotion [ 29 ] further evaluates both recog-nition and reasoning ability with LLM-based judges. Recent emotion MLLMs [ 15 , 16 , 30 , 31 ]increasingly introduce reasoning for better interpretability and reinforcement learning in an attempt to further improve emotion recognition. However, whether reasoning itself truly improves emotion recognition remains unclear. We identify and systematically study the thinking paradox in multimodal emotion reasoning, where fast thinking can outperform slow thinking on recognition, and resolve it through an explicit slow-fast thinking synergy framework. 

3 Understanding the thinking paradox 

This section investigates the counter-intuitive finding that fast thinking outperforms slow thinking in MER tasks (Figure 1 (b)). We show that fast thinking improves recall with more diverse and confident predictions, whereas slow thinking is more conservative, favoring precision by suppressing incorrect categories but lowering confidence in correct ones. Under current evaluation, which mainly relies on recall or F1 metrics, the advantage of slow thinking is not fully reflected. We begin by introducing the emotion reasoning formulation and the emotion-wheel-based evaluation protocol.

3.4 Desiderata for slow-fast thinking synergy 

These observations suggest that an effective emotion reasoning model should not simply prefer one thinking mode over the other. Instead, it should satisfy two desiderata. • Desideratum I : At the prediction level, the model should preserve the recall-oriented coverage of fast thinking while maintaining the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking. • Desideratum II : At the confidence level, the model should preserve fast thinking’s strong confi-dence on correct categories, while retaining slow thinking’s suppression of incorrect ones. These desiderata motivate our slow-fast thinking synergy framework in Section 4. 

4 Method 

We propose MER-R1 , an RL framework for slow-fast thinking synergy. Section 4.1 introduces the GRPO baseline and its limitations. Section 4.2 proposes dual-objective disentanglement to preserve the recall-oriented coverage and the precision-oriented selectivity in the advantage space (Desideratum I). Section 4.3 introduces fast-thinking confidence calibration to strengthen correct categories while suppressing incorrect ones (Desideratum II). The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure 4.

4.2 Dual-objective disentanglement for Desideratum I 

To avoid the ambiguous trade-off induced by a single F1-style reward, we explicitly separate the two optimization goals behind emotion recognition: covering correct categories and avoiding spurious 5ones. We first decompose the task reward into recall and precision rewards, and then compute their group-normalized advantages separately as the optimization signals. 

Reward-level disentanglement. We decompose F1 reward into recall and precision rewards: 

RR = | ˆY∩G| |G| , RP = | ˆY∩G| | ˆY| . (4) This decomposition makes the hidden bi-objective structure of F1 explicit: RR promotes coverage of correct categories, while RP penalizes spurious predictions. Since different recall–precision trade-offs can yield the same F1 value, separating RR and RP avoids an ambiguous optimization target. 

Advantage-level disentanglement. Given the two rewards RR and RP, a straightforward GRPO pipeline would first aggregate them into a scalar reward and then apply group normalization to obtain the advantage. However, when the two rewards have different group-wise variances, the combined scalar reward is dominated by the higher-variance objective, causing the lower-variance objective to be underrepresented in the final advantage. We therefore first compute group-normalized advantages for RR and RP separately then compute the final advantage Adual :

5 Experiments 

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate MER-R1 on two multimodal emotion benchmarks: MER-UniBench [ 11 ] and MME-Emotion [ 29 ]. On MER-UniBench , we follow its three-task setting with 9 datasets: fine-grained emotion recognition on OV-MERD+ [ 12 ], evaluated by the F1-score metric; basic emotion recognition on MER23 [ 7], MER24 [ 8], MELD [ 9], and IEMOCAP [ 10 ], evaluated by 

Hitrate ; and sentiment analysis on MOSI [ 39 ], MOSEI [ 40 ], SIMS [ 41 ], and SIMSv2 [ 42 ], evaluated by Weighted Average F1-score (WAF) . Since Hitrate mainly reflects recall and does not penalize over-prediction, we re-evaluate the four basic emotion recognition datasets using F1-score . We further evaluate on MME-Emotion , which covers 8 tasks: lab, wild, noisy, fine-grained, and multi-label emotion recognition (ER-Lab, ER-Wild, Noise-ER, FG-ER, ML-ER), together with sentiment analysis (SA), fine-grained sentiment analysis (FG-SA), and intent recognition (IR). MME-Emotion reports three metrics: Recognition for emotion recognition, Reasoning for emotion reasoning, and 

CoT as their average. Since the original benchmark used GPT-4o as the evaluator, which is no longer available, we instead use gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview . More details are in Appendix B. 

Implementation details. We use Qwen2.5-Omni [ 3 ] as the backbone and train it on MER-Caption+ [ 11 ] with a two-stage pipeline. We first perform SFT on 5k randomly sampled instances for 2 epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, and then apply RL on the remaining data for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −6, sampling 4 responses per prompt. We set both λcali and λfmt to 0.1.Experiments are conducted on 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. More details are in Appendix C.

6 Conclusion and limitation 

Conclusion. We reveal a thinking paradox in MER, where explicit reasoning does not necessarily improve recognition accuracy. Our analysis shows that fast thinking provides recall-oriented coverage and correct-category confidence, while slow thinking offers precision-oriented selectivity. Based on this complementarity, we propose MER-R1 , an RL framework that realizes slow-fast thinking synergy through dual-objective disentanglement and slow-fast confidence calibration. Experiments on MER-UniBench and MME-Emotion show that MER-R1 achieves SoTA performance and fulfills the Desiderata, making the slow-thinking genuinely beneficial to emotion recognition. 

Limitation. This work is evaluated mainly on MER benchmarks, so its generalization to broader multimodal reasoning tasks remains open [ 43 , 44 ]. Moreover, MER-R1 depends on emotion-wheel-based category mapping and requires an additional fast-thinking forward pass during training, which may limit coverage of fine-grained emotions and increase computational cost. 9References

B Task details 

We evaluate MER-R1 on two multimodal emotion benchmarks: MER-UniBench [ 11 ] and MME-Emotion [ 29 ]. MER-UniBench evaluates open-vocabulary multimodal emotion recognition under emotion-wheel-based metrics, while MME-Emotion provides a llm-as-a-judge based evaluation of both emotion recognition and emotion reasoning. 

B.1 MER-UniBench 

MER-UniBench [ 11 ] unifies nine multimodal emotion datasets into three task groups: fine-grained emotion recognition, basic emotion recognition, and sentiment analysis. Since model outputs are free-form emotion words, direct string matching is insufficient. Following prior work [ 11 , 12 ], we evaluate recognition outputs with an emotion-wheel-based normalization protocol. 

Emotion-wheel normalization. The emotion wheel maps diverse fine-grained emotion words into a shared category space. We apply three normalization steps to both predictions and ground-truth labels. First, morphological variants are reduced to their base form, e.g. , happier and happiness 

are normalized to happy . Second, semantically equivalent emotion words are mapped to the same canonical form, e.g. , joyful and happy . Third, fine-grained emotion words are further mapped to their corresponding inner emotion categories according to each emotion wheel. Following prior work [ 11 ], we adopt K = 5 emotion wheels, as shown in Figure 6. In each wheel, the outer labels correspond to fine-grained emotions, and the inner sectors define the corresponding level-1 emotion categories.

C Implementation details 

We use Qwen2.5-Omni [ 3] as the backbone and train only its Thinker module. All models are trained on MER-Caption+ [ 11 ] with a two-stage SFT-RL pipeline based on GRPO-style optimization. We consider two reasoning baselines. The reproduced AffectGPT-R1 [ 15 ] follows its original SFT/RL data allocation, while keeping the backbone and optimization framework consistent with our implementation. Our stronger Baseline adopts an RL-heavy allocation: we randomly sample 5k MER-Caption+ instances for SFT and use the remaining samples for RL. For this Baseline and 

MER-R1 , we train the SFT stage for 2 epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, and train the RL stage for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −6. During RL, we sample G = 4 responses per prompt, train for 3262 optimization steps, set the batch size to 1, and use gradient accumulation with 2 steps. We set both λcali and λfmt to 0.1. All experiments are conducted on 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, and the RL stage takes approximately 18 hours. For fair comparison, we evaluate all datasets using a single final checkpoint without dataset-specific checkpoint selection. 

D Additional analysis 

Training dynamics on dual rewards. Figure 7 compares the recall and precision reward dynamics of the Baseline and MER-R1 . MER-R1 achieves a substantially higher recall reward, reaching around 

0.75 compared with about 0.67 for the Baseline . Meanwhile, both methods maintain similar precision rewards, fluctuating around 0.65 . This shows that MER-R1 improves recall without sacrificing precision. The dual-objective design therefore better balances the recall–precision trade-off and preserves the recall-oriented coverage that is often suppressed during slow thinking. 

Retained hitrate analysis. We define retained hitrate as the fraction of ground-truth emotion categories that are correctly predicted by fast thinking and still preserved in the final slow-thinking answer. As shown in Figure 8, MER-R1 consistently retains more correct fast-thinking predictions than the Baseline across all five recognition benchmarks. For example, the retained hitrate increases from 83 .5 to 94 .7 on OV-MERD+, from 86 .5 to 95 .4 on MER2023, and from 89 .4 to 97 .2 on MER2024, with similar gains on MELD and IEMOCAP. These results show that MER-R1 better preserves useful fast-thinking intuition during slow thinking, instead of filtering out correct categories as the Baseline often does. This supports the role of slow-fast confidence calibration in selectively retaining recall-oriented signals from fast thinking. 17 E Qualitative analysis 

We provide qualitative examples to further illustrate how MER-R1 realizes slow-fast thinking synergy in multimodal emotion recognition. Specifically, we present three types of cases. (i) Fast-intuition retention refers to cases where fast thinking already predicts a correct emotion, but the baseline slow-thinking answer removes it after deliberation; MER-R1 keeps this useful fast-thinking intuition in the final answer. (ii) Spurious emotion suppression refers to cases where fast thinking introduces incorrect emotions due to over-coverage; MER-R1 suppresses these spurious predictions through slow-thinking selectivity. (iii) Missed emotion recovery refers to cases where baseline fast thinking fails to cover the ground-truth emotion, while MER-R1 recovers it after slow reasoning. For each case type, we show examples from both fine-grained emotion recognition and basic emotion recognition. The fast-intuition retention examples show that MER-R1 does not simply discard the useful predictions produced by fast thinking; instead, it preserves correct categories such as angry 

or happy that are missed by the baseline slow-thinking answer. The spurious emotion suppression examples show that MER-R1 can remove unsupported emotions such as angry or resentment , keeping the final prediction focused on the ground-truth emotions. The missed emotion recovery examples further show that MER-R1 can recover emotions overlooked by baseline fast thinking, such as 

surprised , worried , and other positive fine-grained emotions. Together, these examples qualitatively demonstrate that MER-R1 selectively combines the recall-oriented intuition of fast thinking with the precision-oriented selectivity of slow thinking.
