## Abstract 

In this paper, we propose a Role-aware Stimulus-guided (RASG) framework for interlocutor emotion recognition, which predicts lis-tener emotions from listener-only videos and speaker-only audios. RASG consists of Role-aware Visual Transfer (RVT) and Stimulus-guided Boundary Reasoning (SBR) modules, which address supervi-sion mismatch due to the lack of labeled listener data and ambigu-ity among visually similar listener reactions whose interpretation depends on speaker context, respectively. More specifically, RVT selects speaker samples whose facial expressions support their emo-tion labels. It then filters listener tracks and uses reliable pseudo-labels to train a listener-centric visual expert. SBR uses a two-class language reasoner only when the visual model is uncertain. It treats speaker audio and text as context rather than direct emotion ev-idence to distinguish similar listener reactions. Experiments con-ducted on MER-Cross dataset shows that RASG achieves 76.25% on MER-Cross and improves the performance of the baseline over 17%. Our team ranks second in Track 1 (MER-Cross) of the MER Grand Challenge at ACM MM 2026.

## 1 Introduction 

Modeling semantic relationships between visual content and nat-ural language has been widely explored in multimodal learning [31–34, 38]. In conversational settings, multimodal understanding also involves interpreting the emotions expressed by speakers and the reactions of listeners. Speakers convey affect through words, voice, and facial behavior; listeners show how they receive and respond to these utterances. Interlocutor emotion recognition aims to infer listener emotion from listener video, speaker audio, and the corresponding transcript [27]. The capability supports affect-aware tutoring systems [12] and socially interactive robots [3], which must adapt to users’ emotional responses. The MER series has explored robustness, semi-supervised learning, and large-language-model-based affective understanding [26, 28], while MER-Cross extends the benchmark from individual speakers to interlocutors [27]. MER-Cross defines six listener emotions: neutral, sad, worried, happy, surprise, and angry [27]. Interlocutor emotion recognition poses three challenges: role mismatch, unreliable visual supervision, and distinct roles for lis-tener video and speaker context. First, training labels describe speaker emotions, while predictions target listeners without labeled training examples [27]. Second, speaker emotion labels may reflect prosody or lexical content despite a neutral face, making them unreliable supervision for a listener-visual model. Third, speaker audio and text describe what may have elicited a reaction rather than independently observing the listener’s state. Treating all three modalities as evidence of one person may therefore transfer speaker affect to listener predictions. Speaker-centered conversation models [18, 25, 35] capture temporal and relational context, yet their target emotion remains attached to the active speaker. Semi-supervised ap-proaches [4, 23, 40, 45] can exploit unlabeled data, but errors in the initial model may be reinforced during pseudo-labeling. Language-model-based methods [5, 20, 21, 24] provide contextual emotion reasoning, although unconstrained predictions remain sensitive to prompts and spurious cues [30, 46]. This highlights the importance of selecting and organizing relevant contextual information for multimodal reasoning [29]. The aforementioned limitations cre-ate a role-conditioned visual transfer gap between labeled speaker sources and unlabeled listener targets. Visual supervision from labeled speakers therefore cannot be transferred indiscriminately to listeners, mo-tivating visually grounded source selection and listener-domain bootstrapping. In this paper, we propose a listener-centric framework that learns a visual model without labeled listener data and uses speaker con-text only when the visual evidence is ambiguous. The framework contains two modules: Role-aware Visual Transfer and Stimulus-guided Boundary Reasoning. The first module retains speaker sam-ples whose facial evidence agrees with their emotion labels and uses them to train an initial visual model. It then mines non-speaking listener tracks from an unlabeled conversational pool and accepts pseudo-labels under confidence, temporal, and cross-view consis-tency constraints. The second module activates only for low-margin visual predictions. It represents speaker audio and text as struc-tured cues about the eliciting stimulus and restricts language-model reasoning to the two most plausible visual classes. The framework thus uses context without overriding confident listener-visual pre-dictions. Our contributions are fourfold: 

• We propose a listener-centric framework that combines di-rect listener-visual evidence with speaker stimulus informa-tion for interlocutor emotion recognition when no labeled listener data are available. 

• We devise a Role-aware Visual Transfer module to filter visually inconsistent speaker labels and progressively build listener supervision from unlabeled non-speaking tracks. 

• We develop a Stimulus-guided Boundary Reasoning module that applies pair-constrained language reasoning only to uncertain visual predictions and treats speaker content as eliciting context rather than target-emotion evidence. 

• We conduct extensive experiments on MER-Cross, where RASG achieves 76.25% WAF and outperforms the official CLIP-large baseline by 17.37 percentage points. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Conversational Emotion Recognition and Listener Modeling 

Early conversational emotion recognition methods modeled contex-tual and interpersonal dependencies while predicting the emotion of the current speaker. CMN stored multimodal histories and re-trieved inter-speaker information through attention [14], while ICON encoded self- and inter-speaker influences in shared con-versational memory [13]. DialogueRNN tracked global context, party states, and emotion states [35], and DialogueGCN propagated information over intra- and inter-speaker graph edges [11]. COS-MIC further incorporated commonsense knowledge about mental states, events, and causal relations [10], whereas MMGCN modeled cross-modal and speaker dependencies with graph convolutions [18]. Despite their different context-modeling mechanisms, these methods assume that the utterance and its modalities describe the labeled speaker rather than a listener’s reaction. Listener-centered research has primarily focused on reaction synthesis. Ng et al. [36] generated multiple plausible listener motions from speaker audio and facial motion, while Song et al. [41] modeled listener motion with discrete codewords conditioned on emotional context. Both require paired speaker-listener data to generate reactions rather than recognize emotions in authentic listener videos. ## 2.2 Visual Transfer with Reliable Pseudo-supervision 

Pseudo-labeling assigns model predictions to unlabeled samples as training targets [22], but erroneous predictions may be reinforced. FixMatch improved reliability through confidence-based weak-to-strong consistency [40], while class-balanced pseudo-labeling miti-gated label imbalance in multimodal emotion recognition [4]. How-ever, these methods assume consistent target semantics and do not verify whether an emotion is visually observable or belongs to the correct conversational role. Li et al. [23] selected high-quality emo-tion samples using model agreement and majority voting, which may retain shared systematic biases. TalkNet detected active speak-ers through temporal and cross-modal modeling [42], but speaking status alone cannot determine whether a non-speaking track pro-vides a valid listener reaction or reliable emotion supervision. 

## 2.3 Contextual Emotion Reasoning with Large Language Models 

Recent studies have used large language models for contextual emotion reasoning. Emotion-LLaMA aligned multimodal encoders with a language model through instruction tuning [5], while Af-fectGPT combined multimodal pre-fusion, descriptive annotations, and unified emotion evaluation [24]. Affective-CoT further decom-posed multimodal emotion reasoning into a hierarchical cognitive process [20]. These methods generally reason over the full emotion label space under the assumption that visual, acoustic, and tex-tual evidence all describe the same emotional subject. CausalERC introduced causal prompting for contextual emotion recognition [21], but still predicted the current speaker’s emotion. Zhao et al. [46] corrected prompt-induced label biases using content-free in-puts, yet such general calibration does not impose the role-specific constraints needed to distinguish direct listener evidence from the speaker context that elicits the listener’s reaction.

## 3.3 Role-aware Visual Transfer 

Speaker and listener labels follow different evidence structures. A speaker may express anger prosodically while keeping a neu-tral face; the label is valid for multimodal speaker recognition but noisy for facial learning. Moreover, the unlabeled pool mixes ac-tive speakers and non-speaking listeners. Direct source training thus causes label-evidence mismatch, while pseudo-labeling may assign supervision to the wrong role. RVT addresses both problems by selecting speaker labels supported by facial behavior, training an initial visual teacher, and using it to annotate strictly filtered listener tracks for listener-domain adaptation.

## 3.4 Stimulus-guided Boundary Reasoning 

The visual expert handles prototypical reactions, but difficult sam-ples often lie near class boundaries rather than simply exhibiting weak facial signals: subdued faces may indicate neutral, sad, or worried depending on the preceding interaction. Speaker audio and text describe the eliciting stimulus rather than the target person and cannot serve as direct listener-emotion evidence. SBR there-fore uses context only for uncertain visual predictions and restricts reasoning to the two most plausible visual classes. This can resolve specific visual ambiguity without replacing the visual model with unrestricted language-model inference [5, 20, 24].

## 4 Experiments 4.1 Datasets 

MER-Cross [27]. The MER2026 interlocutor emotion recog-nition track provides 9,395 labeled speaker samples for training and 574 interlocutor samples for testing. Training samples contain speaker video, audio, transcript, and emotion labels; test samples contain listener video with speaker audio and transcript, targeting listener emotion. The six classes are neutral, angry, happy, sad, wor-ried, and surprise. Speaker training labels and listener test labels create a role-conditioned supervision gap. 

MER-SEMI Unlabeled Pool [26]. We use 124,802 unlabeled MER videos without listener annotations. Active-speaker detection retains non-speaking face tracks as candidate listener reactions. The pool serves only listener-domain bootstrapping; MER-Cross test labels are excluded from sample selection, pseudo-labeling, and visual model training. 

## 4.2 Baselines 

HuBERT [16]. The official HuBERT-base baseline captures speaker audio cues rather than directly observing the listener. 

MacBERT [6]. MacBERT-large provides a lexical baseline by encoding speaker transcripts. 

VideoMAE [44]. The official VideoMAE-large baseline uses temporal video features to predict listener emotion. 

ResNet-FER2013 [15, 27]. The FER2013-trained residual net-work provides a frame-level facial-expression baseline. 

EmoNet [43]. The facial affect baseline models categorical emo-tions, action units, and continuous affective dimensions. 

SENet-FER2013 [19, 27]. Channel-wise recalibration strength-ens the FER2013-trained convolutional baseline. 

CLIP [39]. We report CLIP-base and CLIP-large, the strongest official unimodal baseline and our main comparison. 

Top1 Fusion [27]. The baseline combines HuBERT-base audio, MacBERT-large text, and CLIP-large video for a shared prediction. 

Top2 Fusion [27]. The baseline combines HuBERT-large/base, MacBERT-base/large, and CLIP-base/large to assess the benefit of greater multimodal feature diversity.

CLIP ViT-L/14 [39] is the main encoder and is adapted with LoRA [17]; OpenFace [2] remains a fixed facial-behavior extractor; and TalkNet [42] identifies non-speaking tracks in the unlabeled pool. All visual ablations use the same preprocessing and classification protocol. ## 4.3 Quantitative Performance of the Approaches 

Table 1 compares RASG with the official audio-, text-, video-, and multimodal baselines in WAF. ## 4.4 Ablation Studies 

Table 3 evaluates RVT and SBR by adding them progressively to the OpenFace + CLIP reference. Two findings follow. 1) RVT improves WAF from 64.28% to 73.42%, demonstrating the main benefit of role-aligned listener supervision. 2) SBR further raises WAF to 76.25%, showing that speaker context provides complementary gains for low-margin cases. The full framework therefore improves the OpenFace + CLIP reference by 11.97 points. 

## 4.5 Effectiveness of RVT and SBR with the CLIP-large Baseline 

Table 4 decomposes the 17.37-point gain over the CLIP-large base-line. Three findings follow. 1) OpenFace contributes 5.40 points through local facial behavior cues. 2) RVT provides the largest gain of 9.14 points, confirming the importance of listener-domain super-vision. 3) SBR adds 2.83 points by resolving ambiguous low-margin cases without overriding confident visual predictions. The decomposition also clarifies the modules’ complementary roles. RVT yields the larger gain by addressing role-conditioned supervision before listener prediction, while SBR adds a smaller gain on the remaining ambiguous cases. Since SBR operates only on low-margin samples within the visual Top-2 pair, it complements rather than replaces the listener-oriented visual expert. 

## 5 Conclusion 

RASG addresses interlocutor emotion recognition without labeled listener data. RVT filters visually consistent speaker samples and bootstraps pseudo-labeled listener tracks, while SBR uses speaker context only for uncertain visual predictions. RASG achieves 76.25% WAF on MER-Cross, outperforming the official CLIP-large baseline by 17.37 percentage points. RASG achieved second place in Track 1 (MER-Cross) of the MER Grand Challenge at ACM MM 2026, and our results show the complementary value of role-aware visual transfer and constrained stimulus reasoning.
