Abstract 

Recent progress in multimodal, high-dimensional learning has enabled founda-tion models to process heterogeneous, large-scale data. However, at test time, acquiring all features or modalities can be prohibitively costly and often redundant. Sequentially selecting informative modalities is therefore critical, yet challeng-ing when the downstream task or prediction target is unknown. To this end, we introduce ECHO -k, a task-agnostic and self-supervised learning principle for modal-ity acquisition: we use a deep model’s internal pretrained representations (e.g., from a foundation model) as proxy targets that summarize cross-modal informa-tion. We provide theoretical guarantees in a stylized linear setting that motivate a reinforcement learning (RL) policy for sequential modality selection. Across task-agnostic and label-free acquisition baselines, ECHO -k consistently improves budgeted downstream performance across diverse foundation-model backends. Our method provides a principled route to cost-aware test-time deployment, with implications for any multimodal system where measurements are expensive or time-constrained, and downstream tasks unknown a priori . The code is available at § github.com/bunnelab/echo-k .

1 Introduction 

Multimodal foundation models (FMs) thrive on more information: the more modalities we can feed them at test time, the more accurate, robust, and context-aware their representations become (Kaplan et al., 2020; Zhai et al., 2022; Mizrahi et al., 2023; Shukor et al., 2025). In practice, however, not every modality is available by default. Each additional assay, sensor, or data source carries a tangible cost in time, money, throughput, or patient burden. This creates a deployment gap: while models can theoretically produce rich representations by exploiting multiview data, acquiring all views for every instance is infeasible. Modalities are stratified by cost, latency, and availability: in clinical diagnostics, routine measure-ments are often available immediately, while additional molecular or imaging assays can disambiguate uncertain cases but are constrained by turnaround time, cost, or limited sample material. Similar asymmetries arise in scientific discovery, where inexpensive proxies can be collected at scale, but high-fidelity measurements require specialized instrumentation or scarce experimental time. Mea-surements are therefore not acquired exhaustively, nor independently, but as structured groups of modalities or features corresponding to coherent sensing processes. We refer to each such group as a view . Under a fixed budget, only a small number of views can be queried per instance, and the value of each view depends on which views have already been observed. The acquisition problem is therefore inherently sequential and adaptive: at test time, given the views observed so far, which view should a foundation model acquire next to most improve its downstream predictions?

Existing approaches to sequential acquisition are typically task- and data-driven: they define utility with respect to a specific downstream objective, such as classification or regression, and optimize expected task loss (Gong et al., 2019; Gadgil et al., 2023; Guney et al., 2025). This paradigm requires labeled data at acqui-sition time, task-specific reward design, or ex-plicit modeling of task uncertainty. As a re-sult, acquisition policies are tightly coupled to a particular task and dataset, and must be re-trained or redesigned when downstream objec-tives change. This reliance on supervision has left the fully task-agnostic, label-free acquisition setting largely unaddressed (see Related Work in § B). Yet modern pretrained models are in-creasingly deployed as frozen backbones whose representations support many downstream tasks. This shift suggests a different perspective on ac-quisition: rather than selecting measurements for a single supervised objective, one can ask which measurements are needed to preserve the representation formed by the pretrained model. In this work, we propose to use these latent representations learned by modern foundation models as a task-agnostic signal for view acquisition. When trained on heterogeneous, multiview data, these models learn compressed representations that integrate complementary evidence across views into a compact state (Radford et al., 2021; Siméoni et al., 2025; Wenckstern et al., 2025; Oquab et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025; Shmatko et al., 2025). This latent state encodes the information the model deems relevant across many downstream tasks. We use these rich latent states as a learning objective: rather than defining utility with respect to a specific downstream objective, we learn a view selection policy that iteratively selects views to approximate the full latent representation from a minimal number of views. Learning acquisition policies in representation spaces has two main advantages. On the one hand, it removes dependence on downstream supervision: acquisition decisions can be learned without task labels or task-specific losses. On the other hand, it enables broad transfer: because the representation is shared across tasks, preserving it supports many downstream objectives simultaneously. If the partial-view representation closely matches the full-view representation, then any downstream predictor operating on that representation should incur minimal degradation, independent of the task. We instantiate this principle with ECHO -k, a reinforcement learning algorithm for long-horizon sequential view acquisition that modularly integrates with any self-supervised (foundation) model (Fig. 1). At each step, the RL agent selects which view to acquire next and the reward quantifies progress toward the corresponding full-view representation. Once trained, the policy is deployed at inference time on unobserved views to allocate acquisition budgets on an instance-by-instance basis. We support this algorithm with the first theoretical analysis of self-supervised feature acquisition: in a linear model, where we show that representation recovery controls the latent representation regret (Theorem 3.5), and that conditioning on partial observations yields provable gains over fixed orderings. These results in turn justify the RL reward design and motivate the long-horizon acquisition strategy, enabling cost-effective deployment of large multimodal models in settings where full multiview acquisition is impractical. 

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows. 1. We introduce a model-driven framework for self-supervised feature acquisition, in which acquisi-tion decisions are guided by the internal representations of pretrained models, without access to task-specific supervision. 2. We provide the first theoretical analysis of self-supervised feature acquisition. In a stylized linear setting, we derive performance bounds that relate optimal test-time policies and oracle selection strategies, formally characterizing the gap induced by limited information that motivate an RL-based acquisition strategy. 3. We develop ECHO -k, a cost-efficient reinforcement learning algorithm that learns long-horizon acquisition policies using self-supervised latent representations. The policy is trained to maximize 2progress toward the full-view representation, translating the representation-recovery principle into a practical sequential acquisition method. 4. Empirically, we show that ECHO -k learns effective acquisition strategies across diverse data modalities and acquisition horizons. Across image, tabular, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and multimodal geospatial benchmarks, the learned policies rapidly concentrate informative views in early acquisition stages, validating the effectiveness and generality of representation-driven acquisition under realistic budget constraints. Our contributions bridge theory and practice in unsupervised feature acquisition, enabling foundation models to reason about what to measure next under realistic constraints.

From training to test-time deployment. The ideal policy defined above is not deployable at test time. Indeed, it requires both the full-view representation z⋆ and forward passes for every candidate view. During training , we use unlabeled data to construct a self-supervised representation-recovery signal to train a view-selection policy. 4 Methodology 

4.1 From Theoretical Test-Time Policies to Learnable Policies 

The theoretical analysis in § 3 yields three insights that directly inform algorithm design. First, Theorem 3.5 shows that representation-recovery errors control latent regret up to an oracle-mismatch term and a spectral misalignment term, which becomes small when embedding directions are approximately orthogonal. This motivates using progress toward the full-view representation as a self-supervised acquisition signal. Second, the posterior concentration result (Lemma F.4) shows that conditioning on observed views can reduce the gap to a full-information oracle, motivating adaptive, instance-specific policies that update after each acquisition. Third, the analysis is restricted to myopic policies: the oracle itself is one-step greedy, and the regret bound characterizes the gap between two greedy strategies. In practice, the conditional law of unobserved views given Ft is unknown, and acquisition is a finite-horizon decision problem in which early choices affect the value of later measurements, especially when views are correlated (Shim et al., 2018; Zannone et al., 2019; Kachuee et al., 2019; Schütz et al., 2025). This motivates moving beyond the greedy regime to long-horizon planning. Together, these insights culminate in ECHO -k, a reinforcement-learning algorithm for self-supervised, long-horizon view acquisition. We use the theory to define a reward, and learn a parameterized policy 6that optimizes cumulative improvement in representation quality over an entire acquisition trajectory, rather than approximating the one-step oracle. Policy input. At each step, the policy receives the current partial representation zi(P it ), the observed view values with unobserved entries masked, the acquisition mask, and the remaining budget. 5 Experimental Results 

We evaluate ECHO -k across controlled synthetic experiments, vision, and tabular encoders, and three frozen foundation-model settings: single-cell transcriptomics with TABULA (Ding et al., 2025), spatial proteomics with VirTues (Wenckstern et al., 2025), and multimodal geospatial sensing with a self-supervised multimodal encoder (Gordon et al., 2026). The experiments are designed to answer four questions: (i) whether representation-driven acquisition improves downstream performance under limited budgets; (ii) whether the same label-free acquisition principle composes with heterogeneous pretrained backbones; (iii) whether learned acquisition policies transfer to downstream tasks unseen during policy learning; and (iv) whether the method remains effective under realistic deployment constraints, including partially observed reference data and heterogeneous acquisition costs. We discuss the results for foundation models, grouped by application domain, here. Additional controlled benchmarks on synthetic data, and vision datasets such as MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and MiniBooNE, a physics benchmark, along with incorporating costs and adaptive stopping, are shown in § H.

Baselines and metrics. We compare against 12 baselines spanning oracle/random references, model-driven heuristics ( GIR , ESM2-based orderings), and unsupervised feature selection methods (Laplacian Score , SPEC -φ3, MCFS , UDFS , SVD-Ent , Variance ) adapted as acquisition orders (see § G.4 for details). Since most of these baselines induce a fixed, instance-independent acquisition order, we additionally compare against label-free instance-adaptive policies (§ 5.3) and supervised acquisition methods that use downstream labels (§ H.4). 6 Conclusion 

We presented ECHO -k, a task-agnostic and label-free framework for sequential test-time view acqui-sition that uses a pretrained model’s latent representation as a self-supervised proxy: under a cost budget, acquire views so that the induced partial representation rapidly approaches the full-view representation. We support this formulation with a theoretical analysis in a linear setting that charac-terizes the gap between oracle and feasible test-time policies, and we introduce a practical cost-aware RL algorithm that learns long-horizon acquisition strategies. Experiments with controlled synthetic data and multimodal foundation models across single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and geospatial sensing show improved budgeted acquisition, provide evidence for transfer to downstream tasks unseen during policy learning, robustness to partially observed reference data, and extensibility to heterogeneous acquisition costs. The broader implication is that foundation models need not remain passive predictors over pre-collected inputs; they can become resource-aware systems that actively shape the evidence available for inference. 10 Acknowledgments

> ECHO

-k enables multimodal representation models to operate under test-time measurement budgets by learning task-agnostic, label-free acquisition policies. Instead of requiring downstream labels to decide which view to acquire next, the method uses the model’s own full-view representation as a self-supervised reference. This addresses a practical deployment bottleneck: multimodal and scientific foundation models often benefit from additional inputs, but acquiring all modalities can be infeasible due to cost, latency, limited tissue or sample material, scarce instrumentation time, or experimental constraints. In scientific settings, ECHO -k could help prioritize which measurements, assays, markers, sensors, or experiments to run next under limited resources. In clinical settings, it could support budget-aware acquisition of follow-up tests or multimodal patient measurements, while keeping downstream decision-making within validated and expert-supervised workflows. The main limitations concern deployment specificity rather than the general formulation. Acquisition costs and constraints may vary across domains, so practical use requires defining the relevant budget, availability, and cost model for the target setting. The method also benefits from reference representations that are informative enough to guide policy learning; our robustness experiments suggest that these references need not always be complete, but the appropriate level of missingness may depend on the application and backbone. Finally, as with any method intended for scientific or clinical use, policies should be evaluated on the intended endpoint and deployment distribution before adoption. Moreover, 

> ECHO

-k preserves whatever information the pretrained backbone encodes, and does not assert that the full-view representation z⋆ is optimal for every downstream task; any task-irrelevant information in z⋆ is therefore inherited from the backbone. Our experiments evaluate transfer across downstream objectives, but do not test explicit distribution shift between the reference-construction data and the deployment domain. Similarly, the full-view representation is not always the best training target: masked references outperform it on MNIST-14 at 20% and 40% budgets and on hPancreas at 20% (§ H), suggesting that masking can act as a mild regularizer during training. Our robustness sweep considers randomly masked reference views and does not identify where partial references fail, since even at 50% masking ECHO -k remains competitive with strong baselines. A natural extension is structured incompleteness, where an entire assay or modality is missing for a subset of samples, and understanding how the reference representation interacts with that structure is an interesting direction for future work. These considerations leave substantial room for future work on richer cost models, uncertainty-aware acquisition, and prospective validation in real-world acquisition workflows. 

B Related Work 

Classical feature selection methods aim to find a subset of relevant features that maximizes model performance while excluding irrelevancies (Kohavi & John, 1997; Guyon & Elisseeff, 2003). Early surveys (Blum & Langley, 1997) reviewed both empirical advances and theoretical definitions of feature relevance, formalizing the notions of strong and weak relevance. Several works cast the problem as submodular optimization (Killamsetty et al., 2021; Jain et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2014; Coleman et al., 2019), though their end goal is to improve performance under limited budget during training rather than at test time. Previous work has also centered around greedy heuristics with theoretical guarantees for sequential search (Golovin & Krause, 2011). Another line of work focuses on instance-specific or personalized feature selection (Melville et al., 2004; Li et al., 2018). Beyond selecting a subset of features, a related field is feature importance quantification : given a fixed dataset and (most commonly) a target, the goal is to isolate features that contribute significantly to predictions (Guyon & Elisseeff, 2003; Settles, 2009; Li et al., 2017; Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Sristi et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2023; Lifshitz et al., 2025). Model-specific methods such as (Breiman, 2001) leverage the structure of the model to assign importance ranks, while model-agnostic methods (Fisher et al., 2019) rely on importance metrics applicable to any model. 

Active learning (AL) incorporates a sample selection policy in the training loop in an online fashion: at each time t, a new sample is queried to augment the existing dataset (Settles, 2009; Zhan et al., 2022; Tharwat & Schenck, 2023; Li et al., 2025). AL methods are particularly well suited for settings where acquiring new samples is expensive. They can be broadly split into three types of strategies (Zhan et al., 2022). Uncertainty-based querying strategies select points based on the estimated information they provide to the learning algorithm, including maximum entropy selection (Shannon, 1948), confidence-based selection (Wang & Shang, 2014), and adversarial methods (Ducoffe & Precioso, 2018). Diversity-based strategies aim to increase the coverage of samples in the dataset, 18 for instance through core-set approaches that build subsets approximating the full dataset (Sener & Savarese, 2017; Claici et al., 2018; Feldman, 2019; Kim & Shin, 2022). Lastly, combined strategies mix both approaches (Yin et al., 2017; Zhdanov, 2019; Shui et al., 2020). 

Feature acquisition (FA). Our method focuses on designing instance-specific feature acquisition strategies rather than sample acquisition policies (Settles, 2009; Kirsch et al., 2019; Holzmüller et al., 2023; Kolossov et al., 2023; Gupte et al., 2024). FA strategies can be broadly split (Rahbar et al., 2025) into greedy strategies that sequentially acquire information by maximizing expected utility, and non-greedy methods that plan acquisition over multiple time steps (Zubek & Dietterich, 2002; Dulac-Arnold et al., 2011; Shim et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2018, 2025; Norcliffe et al., 2025; Piskorz et al., 2025), often using reinforcement learning (Shim et al., 2018; Zannone et al., 2019; Kachuee et al., 2019). This problem has also been studied under the name of Bayesian experiment design 

(BED), which aims to optimize the distribution of ξ, the random variable controlling the experiment, in order to maximize the information gain obtained by running the experiment parametrized by 

ξ (Chaloner & Verdinelli, 1995; Sebastiani & Wynn, 2000; Cavagnaro et al., 2010; Ryan et al., 2016; Rainforth et al., 2024). Classical BED methods sequentially update a model from a stream of incoming experiments; recently, the focus has shifted towards policy learning (Foster et al., 2021; Blau et al., 2022, 2023) and decision-aware experiment design (Huang et al., 2024). 

Task-agnostic acquisition. Contrarily to previous work, our proposed method is fundamentally task-agnostic. While some active learning and feature selection strategies have considered weakly or unlabeled settings (Ash et al., 2019; Yehuda et al., 2022; Lifshitz et al., 2025; Kobayashi et al., 2025), task-agnostic feature acquisition has received little attention. Closest to our setup are Li & Oliva (2021), who consider a partially supervised setup and tackle this problem by training a surrogate reward model, and introduce an active instance recognition task in which the goal is to select features to reduce uncertainty. 

Theoretical aspects of FA have received little attention, both in the supervised and unsupervised setting. Chen et al. (2015) study a probabilistic model inspired by Bayesian experiment design, in which the goal is to learn a policy that sequentially acquires features to infer the value of a target. Kobayashi et al. (2025) and Covert et al. (2023) provide theoretical insights in the supervised case. Our work is the first to consider the unsupervised case and to provide a bound on the gap between the greedy oracle and the optimal test-time policy. 

C Extended Mathematical Framework 

Data generation. We consider n i.i.d. instances. For each i ∈ [[1 , n ]] , the data xi = ( mi

is drawn from a joint distribution over M1 × · · · × M K . We use view , modality , and feature group 

interchangeably, as our framework applies uniformly to all settings. 

Representation model. Each view mk is processed by a view-specific encoder Φk : Mk → Rdk .The encoded views are combined through a fixed aggregation function AW : Rd1 × · · · × RdK → Rp,yielding the full-view representation z∗ = AW(Φ 1(m1), . . . , ΦK (mK )) . This subsumes both multimodal models (each mk is a distinct modality: image, audio, protein marker, . . . ) and unimodal models (features partitioned into jointly acquired groups processed by a shared encoder). For notational simplicity, we drop the encoders in subsequent expressions and treat views as directly mapped into the latent space. 

Sequential acquisition. Let Pt ⊆ [[1 , K ]] denote the acquired set after t steps and z(Pt) := 

AW({Φk(mk) : k ∈ Pt}) the induced partial representation, with unobserved views treated as missing. The finite-horizon acquisition problem is to select a sequence (k1, . . . , k T ), with kt+1 /∈ Pt,minimizing the representation-recovery loss 

L(Pt) := ∥z(Pt) − z⋆∥22.

Equivalently, the policy maximizes cumulative progress toward z⋆ along the acquisition trajectory. The ideal one-step greedy rule is 

kt+1 ∈ arg min 

> k / ∈Pt

∥z(Pt ∪ { k}) − z∗∥22 , (6) which is not deployable at test time, since it requires access to both z⋆ and the representation after acquiring each candidate view. 19 Markov decision process formulation. We frame sequential acquisition as an instance-specific MDP with a continuous state space and evolving action set. Dropping the instance superscript for clarity, the components are: • State. At step t, the state is st = ( Mt, xPt , z(Pt), b t), where Mt ∈ { 0, 1}K is the acquisi-tion mask, xPt are the observed view values, z(Pt) is the current partial representation, and 

bt is the remaining budget. To maintain a fixed state space across steps, unobserved views are set to NA , so the augmented space is  M1 ∪ { NA } × · · · ×  MK ∪ { NA } (Norcliffe et al., 2025). • Actions. The available actions at step t are the unacquired views: A(st) = {k ∈ [[1 , K ]] : 

k / ∈ Pt}.• Transitions. Upon selecting kt+1 , its value is drawn from mkt+1 ∼ pdata (· | mk, k ∈ Pt),the state updates to Pt+1 = Pt ∪ { kt+1 }, and z(Pt+1 ) is recomputed. The transition probability between states differing only in coordinate kt is 

P[Pt | Pt−1] = P[at = kt | Pt−1] · pdata (mkt | mk, k ∈ Pt−1) .

• Reward. The reward at step t + 1 quantifies progress toward the reference-view representa-tion: 

rt+1 (Pt+1 ) := ∥z(Pt) − zref ∥22 − ∥ z(Pt+1 ) − zref ∥22.

where zref can be the full view embedding z∗ or the embedding from all the views (can be a subset of full views) measured for the underlying data point. 

Selection policies. A stochastic selection policy is a map πθ (· | st) ∈ ∆( A(st)) , where ∆( A(st)) 

is the probability simplex over available actions. During training, fully observed samples allow z⋆ to be computed and used as a self-supervised target — no downstream labels are required. When only a reference subset Ri is observed for instance i, z(Ri) serves as a partial target. At deployment, the learned policy is fixed and acts solely from the current state st.

Connection to sequential decision making. This MDP is an instance of a K-armed bandit with correlated arms and a single-pull constraint per arm (Gupta et al., 2021); see also Guha & Munagala (2007); Chakrabarti et al. (2008); Carrara et al. (2019) for related setups. The sequential decision problem is Markovian with respect to (Pt, z(Pt)) : given the current partial representation and acquired set, future acquisition values are independent of the full history of selections.

G Experimental Details 

G.1 Datasets 

We evaluate ECHO -k and all baselines on six datasets spanning seven downstream tasks. Table 5 provides a summary. Below we describe each dataset in detail, including the downstream task, preprocessing choices, and the specific aspects that make it relevant for evaluating budgeted feature acquisition. 

G.1.1 Vision Datasets: MNIST and Fashion-MNIST 

MNIST (LeCun et al., 1998) and Fashion-MNIST (Xiao et al., 2017) are standard computer vision benchmarks consisting of images of handwritten digits and Zalando article images, respectively. Both have 10-class classification as the downstream task. Following Li & Oliva (2021), we downsample images from 28 × 28 to 14 × 14 resolution, treating each pixel as an independently acquirable feature and yielding d = 196 acquisition units. This downsampling increases the information density per pixel and makes the acquisition problem more challenging, since fewer pixels suffice to reach a given accuracy. We use a 70:15:15 split (50k/10k/10k). To ensure a controlled comparison, all acquisition methods are initialized from the same top-corner pixel ( ), so differences in accuracy reflect only the quality of the subsequent acquisition strategy. 

G.1.2 Physics: MiniBooNE 

The MiniBooNE dataset (Roe, 2005) originates from the Fermilab MiniBooNE neutrino experiment. The goal is binary classification: distinguishing electron-neutrino signal events from muon-neutrino background events. The dataset contains d = 33 reconstructed physics variables derived from detector signals, including Cherenkov ring characteristics, event energy, and track reconstruction outputs. In practice, computing these reconstructed variables from raw detector data is expensive and the variables exhibit substantial mutual correlations, making it undesirable to rely on the full feature set at test time. We adopt the preprocessing protocol of Norcliffe et al. (2025), which standardizes features and removes duplicate events, and use a 70:15:15 split (65k/5k/6k). This dataset is particularly relevant for evaluating ECHO -k because the strong inter-feature correlations create a setting where greedy, myopic acquisition can be suboptimal: selecting the individually most informative variable first may be redundant with the second-most informative one, motivating the long-horizon planning that ECHO -k provides. 

G.1.3 Single-Cell Transcriptomics: hPancreas 

The hPancreas dataset (Ding et al., 2025) is a curated single-cell RNA-sequencing collection from human pancreatic tissue with 14 cell type labels spanning endocrine, exocrine, and immune popula-tions. This dataset is widely used as a benchmark in the single-cell foundation model literature. From 28 the 1,200 genes available, we retain the top 200 most variable genes using Cell Ranger (Zheng et al., 2017) and ScanPy (Wolf et al., 2018), following standard practice for reducing noise from uninforma-tive transcripts while preserving biologically meaningful variation. We use the preprocessing, gene filtering, and data splits provided by (Ding et al., 2025) (9.5k/1k/4.2k). Cell-type annotation serves as a representative downstream task: it is the most common evaluation objective in single-cell analysis and requires the acquired gene subset to preserve fine-grained phenotypic distinctions between closely related cell populations. Crucially, the ECHO -k policy is trained without any access to cell-type labels; the annotation task is used purely for post-hoc evaluation of the acquired gene panels. 

G.1.4 Spatial Proteomics: NSCLC Patient Cohort 

The NSCLC clinical cohort (Cords et al., 2024) consists of spatial proteomics data acquired via imaging mass cytometry (IMC) at 1 μm/px resolution with d = 40 semantically meaningful protein markers. The marker panel includes tumor markers (e.g., panCK ), proliferation markers (e.g., Ki-67 ), immune cell markers (e.g., CD4 , CD8 , CD68 ), stromal markers, and vascular markers, providing a comprehensive view of the tumor microenvironment. Clinical annotations include cancer subtyping (2 classes: adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma) and cancer grading (3 classes), the latter being substantially more challenging due to subtler morphological and molecular differences between grades. We adopt the preprocessing pipeline described in (Wenckstern et al., 2025), which includes cell segmentation, marker normalization, and quality filtering. The experimental setup is inherently sequential: ECHO -k iteratively selects one protein marker at a time, the VirTues backbone updates its tissue embedding given the newly observed marker, and the updated embedding informs the next acquisition decision. This mirrors the real-world workflow in multiplexed imaging, where tissue sections are characterized through successive rounds of antibody staining and each additional round carries a cost. The acquisition budget is set to T = 10 markers (25% of the full panel), reflecting realistic constraints on antibody panel size in clinical multiplex imaging. 

G.1.5 Geospatial Earth Observations: MMEarth-Bench 

The MMEarth-Bench dataset (Gordon et al., 2026) is a global multimodal Earth-observation bench-mark to evaluate models under heterogeneous sensing conditions. Each location is represented by 12 aligned geospatial views, including optical satellite imagery, radar measurements, terrain and elevation products, land-cover maps, canopy-height information, meteorological variables, and geolocation metadata. It provides a multimodal acquisition setting: each view corresponds to a distinct sensing product or environmental data source that may differ in availability, cost, spatial resolution, and information content. We treat each modality group as one acquisition unit and pair 

> ECHO

-k with a frozen multimodal geospatial representation model trained on the full set of available views. We evaluate downstream transfer on soil pH regression, reporting R2 as a function of the acquired modality budget.

G.2 Architectures and Backbone Details G.2.1 Masked Autoencoder (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, MiniBooNE) 

For image and tabular data, we train a masked autoencoder via self-supervised learning on the full dataset. Given an input x, a random subset of features is revealed via a binary mask m ∈ { 0, 1}d,and the model learns an encoder z = fθ (x ⊙ m, m ) and decoder ˆx = gθ (z) to reconstruct the full input using an MSE objective. The encoder receives both the masked input and the mask itself, allowing it to distinguish between zero-valued features and unobserved ones. The masking ratio is sampled uniformly during training to expose the model to a wide range of partial observations. After pretraining, the encoder is frozen and used as the representation backbone for ECHO -k. For downstream evaluation, a linear probe is trained on z for classification under random masking to simulate partial observations; this ensures that the probe is calibrated to the type of partial inputs encountered during sequential acquisition. The same frozen encoder is shared across all baselines and ECHO -k.

G.2.2 TABULA Foundation Model 

For single-cell RNA-seq, we use the pretrained TABULA foundation model (Ding et al., 2025), a self-supervised transformer designed for single-cell transcriptomic data. TABULA is trained on large-scale single-cell atlases and can embed partially observed gene expression profiles into cell-level representations. When a subset of genes is unobserved, TABULA treats these entries as missing and produces an embedding conditioned only on the available genes. This native support for partial observations makes it well-suited as a backbone for ECHO -k. The downstream task of cell-type annotation is evaluated via a linear probe trained on top of the cell embeddings. We use TABULA without any fine-tuning; the model weights are frozen throughout both policy learning and evaluation. 

G.2.3 VirTues Foundation Model 

For the NSCLC patient cohort, we deploy ECHO -k with the VirTues spatial proteomics foundation model (Wenckstern et al., 2025). VirTues is a graph-based transformer that operates on cell-level protein expression profiles within tissue sections, producing both cell-level and tissue-level repre-sentations. Tissue-level representations are obtained by averaging the cell-level summary tokens across all cells in the tissue sample. When a protein marker is unobserved, its expression value is treated as missing in the cell-level input, and VirTues produces an embedding conditioned on the available markers. Linear probes are trained for cancer subtyping and grading using the tissue-level embeddings. Importantly, the same ECHO -k policy is used for both downstream tasks: the acquisition order is determined once by the policy and then evaluated on both tasks, demonstrating task-agnostic panel design. 

G.2.4 GeoSpatial Encoder 

For MMEarth-Bench, a masked encoder is used. Concretely, each of the 12 Earth-observation modalities is first converted to a modality-level vector by global average pooling for spatial inputs and identity for tile-level inputs, then projected to a shared dimension and multiplied by a binary 30 modality mask indicating which modalities are observed. The masked per-modality projections are concatenated together with the mask itself and passed through an MLP encoder to produce a latent representation z. This encoder is trained under random modality masking, so it learns to make reconstructions from arbitrary partial modality subsets while explicitly distinguishing unobserved modalities from zero-valued features. 

Backbone-agnosticism. Across all settings, both ECHO -k and the baselines have access only to the pretrained models during the feature selection phase. Linear probes are used exclusively for downstream evaluation after feature acquisition. ECHO -k interfaces with each backbone only through the partial embedding z(St) and the full-information target z⋆; no architecture-specific modifications are needed. This design means that ECHO -k can be deployed with any new backbone simply by providing the embedding function, without rewriting any acquisition logic. 

G.3 Evaluation Protocol 

Each dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test splits. For image and tabular tasks, we use a 70:15:15 split. For cell-type annotation, we adopt the predefined splits released with TABULA (Ding et al., 2025), and for cancer subtyping and grading, we follow the test splits defined in (Wenckstern et al., 2025). 

Acquisition curves. The primary evaluation metric is the acquisition curve: for each method, we plot downstream performance (MSE or classification accuracy) as a function of the number of features acquired t ∈ [[1 , d ]] . This allows us to compare methods across the full range of acquisition budgets and identify regimes where differences are most pronounced. In practice, the early regime (5–25% of features) is of greatest interest, since this is where cost savings are largest. 

Linear probes. For all real-world datasets, downstream performance is evaluated by training a linear probe on the partial embeddings z(St) produced by the frozen backbone. A single linear probe is trained per downstream task using random masking augmentation and held fixed across acquisition budgets. This ensures that the evaluation is task-specific (it depends on the downstream labels) while the acquisition policy remains task-agnostic. 

Reproducibility. All experiments are run with three random seeds for both the ECHO -k policy and the downstream evaluation. The Random baseline is additionally averaged over five independent runs per seed to reduce variance. Error bands in all figures correspond to standard error across seeds.

G.5.1 MDP Formulation 

Let d denote the total number of features and T the acquisition budget. An episode corresponds to sequentially selecting T features without replacement. At step t, the agent has observed a subset 

St ⊆ { 1, . . . , d }. We define a finite-horizon Markov decision process (S, A, P, r ) as follows. 

State. The observable state summarizes the acquisition history and the induced latent representation: 

st := ( St, z(St), t/T, ∥z(St)∥2, x ⊙ mt)

where mt ∈ { 0, 1}d is the binary acquisition mask corresponding to St, and z(St) denotes the latent embedding produced by the frozen self-supervised encoder given the partially observed input. This state representation renders the process Markov with respect to the surrogate objective. 

Action. At each step, the agent selects a feature index at ∈ A t := {1, . . . , d } \ St, corresponding to acquiring a previously unobserved feature. We apply invalid action masking (Huang & Ontañón, 2020), assigning zero probability to actions in St during policy evaluation and optimization. 

Transition. Transitions are deterministic: selecting at = j yields St+1 = St ∪ { j} and updates the latent embedding to z(St+1 ).

G.5.3 Policy Learning 

We learn a stochastic policy πθ (at | st) using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) with invalid action masking (Huang & Ontañón, 2020). During both training and inference, the policy has access only to the frozen self-supervised encoder and the induced latent representations. Downstream task labels and linear probes are used exclusively for post-hoc evaluation and do not influence the policy or reward.

H.8 Adaptive Stopping 

A fixed acquisition horizon may be restrictive in deployment, where the policy should also decide whether the currently acquired views are already sufficient. We extend ECHO -k with a STOP action, converting the fixed-horizon MDP into an optimal-stopping problem. For a measurement action a,the reward is
