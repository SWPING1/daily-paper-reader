Abstract —Pain is a multifaceted and widespread phenomenon with substantial clinical and societal burden, making reliable automated assessment a critical objective. This paper presents a lightweight transformer architecture that fuses multiple fNIRS representations through a unified tokenization mechanism, en-abling joint modeling of complementary signal views without requiring modality-specific adaptations or increasing architec-tural complexity. The proposed token-mixing strategy preserves spatial, temporal, and time-frequency characteristics by projecting heterogeneous inputs onto a shared latent representation, using a structured segmentation scheme to control the granularity of local aggregation and global interaction. The model is evaluated on the AI4Pain dataset using stacked raw waveform and power spectral density representations of fNIRS inputs. Experimental results demonstrate competitive pain recognition performance while remaining computationally compact, making the approach suitable for real-time inference on both GPU and CPU hardware. 

Index Terms —Pain assessment, deep learning, neural signals, data fusion 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Pain serves as an evolutionary function by signaling potential tissue damage or disease to the organism, thereby helping preserve physiological integrity [1]. It is inherently subjective and multidimensional, involving nociceptive, sensory, affective, and cognitive components [2]. Due to its high prevalence and substantial societal burden, pain has been described as a “silent public health crisis ” [3], emphasizing the need for systematic and reliable assessment. Opioid analgesics remain the most frequently prescribed treatment for pain [4], despite their well-documented risks of addiction, overdose [5], and side effects such as sedation, depression, anxiety, and nausea that severely impair daily functioning and quality of life [6]. The subjective and complex nature of pain continues to challenge accurate assessment in both clinical practice and research. Reliance on self-reported measures limits treatment precision and contributes to overprescription and misuse of analgesics. These difficulties are particularly pronounced in medically unstable patients or those unable to communicate effectively [7]. Pain in critically ill adults is often under-recognized and inadequately managed, mainly due to the scarcity of objective, standardized assessment tools [8]. Cancer-related pain is especially common, particularly in advanced stages of the disease [9]. In addition, pain directly affects attention, functional performance, and cognitive processing speed [10]. For instance, Li et al. [11] showed that pain disrupts cognitive control by impairing expectancy processing and decision formation. Moore et al. [12] demonstrated that both chronic and experimentally induced pain alter attentional performance in a task-dependent manner. Pain assessment strategies span multiple modalities, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Self-report instruments, such as numerical rating scales and standardized questionnaires, remain the clinical gold standard because they directly capture the subjective experience of pain. However, their reliability is limited in patients who cannot communicate effectively, including critically ill, sedated, cognitively impaired, or pedi-atric populations. In such cases, behavioral observation methods based on facial expressions, vocalizations, and body movements

are used to infer pain levels [13]. Physiological signals, including electrocardiography and skin conductance, have also been explored as objective indicators of nociceptive responses [14]. However, these measures often reflect general stress or arousal rather than pain-specific neural activity, motivating the use of neuroimaging approaches capable of directly capturing cortical processes associated with pain perception. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a promising noninvasive neuroimaging modality for cap-turing cortical activity related to pain. It monitors changes in cerebral hemodynamics and oxygenation by simultaneously measuring concentrations of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) in the cortex [15]. Studies have consistently shown that noxious stimuli elicit distinct oxygenation changes across multiple cortical regions in both healthy subjects and clinical populations [16]. This work explores brain activity using fNIRS, evaluat-ing two representations: the original 1D multichannel wave-forms and their time-frequency spectrograms. We introduce a lightweight transformer-based architecture that performs multi-stream, multi-representation fusion through a specialized token-mixing mechanism, achieving strong performance while maintaining computational efficiency. II. R ELATED WORK 

Over the last decade, research on automatic pain assessment has grown rapidly. This progress has been accompanied by a transition from classical image- and signal-processing pipelines to deep learning-based approaches, although conventional methods still perform well, especially when data are limited [17]. Most existing methods focus on video, aiming to capture behavioral manifestations of pain through facial expressions, body motion, and other observable cues, and they employ a broad range of learning strategies [18]–[21]. Although video-based approaches dominate the field, an increasing number of studies have also investigated biosignal-driven pain recognition, using physiological markers from electrocardiography [22], [23], electromyography [24]–[26], electrodermal activity (EDA) [27]–[32], and brain activity measured with functional near-infrared spectroscopy [15], [16], [33]–[36]. Several pain studies have employed fNIRS in conjunction with machine learning to extract informative features and discriminate between pain con-ditions. In [37], a bag-of-words representation combined with a K-NN classifier on time–frequency features outperformed using time- or frequency-domain features alone. In contrast, [38] reported the best results by combining time- and frequency-domain features with a Gaussian SVM, while [33] used raw fNIRS signals with a two-layer BiLSTM, achieving 90 .60% 

accuracy in a multi-class classification task. Finally, [39] proposed a hybrid CNN–LSTM architecture to capture spatio-temporal patterns in fNIRS and reported high performance. Multimodal pain recognition has also gained momentum, with evidence that combining behavioral and physiological information can improve robustness and predictive performance [40]–[42]. In [43], the authors fused features extracted from ECG and EDA and applied tree-based classifiers for pain recognition. Similarly, [44] combined wavelet-based features from ECG and EMG and used a k-nearest neighbor classifier. Beyond biosignals, [45] integrated facial and voice features in pediatric postoperative settings, reporting strong performance and further underscoring the value of multimodal modeling. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

This section details the preprocessing pipeline for fNIRS signals, describes the augmentation and regularization strategies used for pain recognition, and presents the proposed transformer model, along with its underlying tokenization approach. 

A. Pre-processing 

In the preprocessing stage, the fNIRS signals are utilized in their original waveform format (as multichannel 1D vectors) and also as spectrogram representations derived from the raw signals. These spectrograms are computed via short-time power spectral density (PSD) estimation over successive sliding windows, yielding 2D time-frequency representations that are resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The proposed methodology can handle both filtered and unfiltered raw signals, as it does not rely on hand-crafted feature engineering. However, in cases of heavy noise contamination, a denoising step may be beneficial. In this study, unfiltered signals are employed. 

B. Architecture 

The architecture uses the unified token representation produced by the proposed tokenization method, allowing a single model to process inputs with different dimensionalities, structures, or modalities. A set of learnable latent vectors interacts with the input token sequence through cross-attention and is iteratively refined using self-attention and feedforward layers [46]. This design preserves a fixed latent dimensionality while adapting to variations in input resolution, sequence length, and modality. The approach imposes a structured latent bottleneck by partitioning the token sequence into segments and associating a latent vector with each segment. Specifically, for an input tensor:

This study utilizes the AI4Pain dataset [47], [48], comprising synchronized facial videos and fNIRS recordings from 65 

participants. The dataset was gathered at the Human–Machine Interface Laboratory at the University of Canberra, Australia, and divided into 41 subjects for training, 12 for validation, and 

12 for testing. To induce controlled pain stimuli, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation electrodes were placed on the inner forearm and the dorsum of the right hand. Pain threshold denotes the lowest stimulus intensity perceived as painful (low pain), whereas pain tolerance denotes the highest intensity a participant can tolerate before it becomes unbearable (high pain). For the fNIRS modality, 24 oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) channels were employed. All experiments use a three-class classification scheme (No Pain, Low Pain, High Pain) and are evaluated under a hold-out strategy on the validation set, reporting macro-averaged accuracy, precision, and F1 score. Comparisons with previous methods on the testing set are detailed in Section V.

V. C OMPARISON WITH EXISTING METHODS 

Table V presents a comparison of the AI4Pain testing set across video-based, fNIRS-based, and multimodal ap-proaches. Classical handcrafted-feature pipelines have limited capacity to capture complex pain-related patterns, with the SVM framework in [47] achieving at most 43 .30% accuracy across modalities. Deep learning methods yield consistent improvements: the multimodal transformer in [49] achieves 

46 .67% , followed by a video-only CNN at 49 .00% [50], and a CNN–Transformer fusion at 51 .33% [51]. The ensemble classifier in [52] reaches 53 .66% using handcrafted fNIRS features, while the video transformer in [53] achieves 55 .00% .The multimodal transformer in [54] reports the strongest result at 55 .69% through joint video–fNIRS learning. The proposed method achieves 50 .00% accuracy on the testing set using stacked waveform and PSD fNIRS representations. Relying exclusively on cortical signals without any behavioral input, this result surpasses all classical fNIRS pipelines and remains competitive with multimodal systems, supporting the viability of unified tokenization and representation fusion for fNIRS-based pain recognition. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This work presented a lightweight transformer for fNIRS-based pain recognition, built around a unified tokenization and latent segmentation strategy that supports multi-representation fusion without architectural specialization. Evaluated on the 

AI4Pain dataset, the model achieves stable recognition perfor-mance across a broad range of computational configurations, with segment numbers between 2 and 16 offering the most favorable balance between efficiency and accuracy.
